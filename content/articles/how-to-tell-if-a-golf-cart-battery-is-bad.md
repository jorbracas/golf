A golf cart battery is on its way out when **range drops noticeably, the cart slows on hills it used to climb, or one battery in the pack reads clearly lower than the others after a full charge.** Usually one or two batteries fail first and drag the rest of the pack down, so the job is to find which ones.

You need a basic digital multimeter. A hydrometer helps with flooded batteries. Wear safety glasses and gloves: batteries contain acid and can produce hydrogen gas while charging.

## The warning signs

- The cart won't finish 18 holes, or a route it used to manage easily (see what [normal range](/shop/how-far-can-a-golf-cart-go) looks like)
- It feels sluggish or "dies" going uphill, then recovers on the flat
- The charger runs much longer than usual or never seems to finish
- One battery is hotter than the others after charging
- Swollen cases, cracks or leaking acid
- Heavy white or green corrosion that keeps coming back

## Test 1: resting voltage

Charge fully, unplug, and let the cart sit for at least a few hours, ideally overnight. Then measure each battery across its posts.

| Battery | Fully charged at rest | About 50% charged |
|---|---|---|
| 6 V | ~6.3–6.4 V | ~6.0 V |
| 8 V | ~8.4–8.5 V | ~8.0 V |
| 12 V | ~12.7–12.8 V | ~12.2 V |

A fully charged pack should be within roughly 0.1–0.2 V per battery. **One battery noticeably below the others is your prime suspect.**

## Test 2: the drive test

Voltage at rest can hide a weak battery. Load reveals it.

1. Record each battery's voltage after a full charge.
2. Drive the cart for 10–15 minutes, including a hill if you can.
3. Measure each battery again straight away.

The healthy batteries all drop by about the same amount. A failing one drops much further. If you have a carbon-pile load tester, it does the same job more precisely.

## Test 3: specific gravity (flooded batteries)

With a hydrometer, draw electrolyte from each cell after a full charge:

- **About 1.265–1.280:** healthy, fully charged
- **A cell 0.050 or more below the others:** that cell is failing

Only test after topping up and recharging. Testing straight after adding water gives false low readings.

## Test 4: check the simple stuff first

Before you buy batteries, rule out the cheap causes:

- **Loose or corroded cables and terminals.** Clean them with a baking-soda solution and tighten them to spec.
- **Low water** in flooded batteries. Exposed plates sulphate quickly. Top up with distilled water after charging, just above the plates.
- **A faulty charger** that never fully charges the pack. Check the pack voltage at the end of a charge cycle.

## Test 5: the charger tells you something too

If the charger shuts off quickly on a pack that's clearly flat, or never shuts off at all, either the charger or a battery is faulty. Try a known-good charger if you can borrow one. If your cart came with a "smart" charger that needs some pack voltage to start, a very flat pack may need the steps in our [dead battery charging guide](/blog/how-to-charge-a-dead-golf-cart-battery).

## Test 6: age and history

Flooded lead-acid packs typically last **around 4–6 years**, less if they were stored discharged, run flat often or left dry. If your pack is that old and shows the signs above, more testing probably won't change the conclusion.

## Replace one battery or the whole pack?

- **Pack under ~2 years old:** replacing the single bad battery is reasonable.
- **Older pack:** a new battery among tired ones gets unbalanced charging and dies early. Replace the set.
- **Replacing the set anyway:** compare the cost with a [lithium conversion](/blog/convert-golf-cart-to-lithium-battery). Lithium often makes sense at this point: no watering, about twice the usable range and hundreds of pounds less weight.

If a battery is only mildly sulphated, [reconditioning](/blog/how-to-recondition-golf-cart-batteries) can sometimes buy time, but it doesn't save a battery with a dead cell.
