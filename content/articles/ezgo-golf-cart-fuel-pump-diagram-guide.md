E-Z-GO gas carts use a small **pulse-driven diaphragm fuel pump**. It has no wires and no motor. Pressure pulses from the engine's crankcase flex a diaphragm, which pulls fuel from the tank and pushes it to the carburettor. There are **three hoses**, and getting them in the right place solves a surprising number of "won't start" problems.

## The hose diagram

![Diagram: fuel flows from the tank through a filter into the pump IN port and out of the OUT port to the carburettor; a separate pulse line connects the engine crankcase to the pump](/images/diagrams/ezgo-fuel-pump-hoses.svg "Fuel goes tank → filter → pump → carburettor. The pulse line only carries pressure pulses from the crankcase.")

| Port | Connects to | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulse / vacuum** | Engine crankcase fitting | Carries the pressure pulses that drive the diaphragm. No fuel goes through it. |
| **Inlet (IN)** | Fuel tank pickup, usually via an inline filter | Fuel is drawn in here |
| **Outlet (OUT)** | Carburettor inlet | Fuel is pushed out here |

Most pumps have an arrow or IN/OUT marks moulded into the body. If yours is unmarked, blow gently through each fuel port with the pump off the cart: air should only pass in the direction of flow.

On carts with Kawasaki engines (most gas TXT, RXV, Express and Valor models from the late 2000s on), the pump sits under the seat or behind the rear access panel near the engine. Older carts with Robin engines use the same principle, but the pump and fittings differ. Match the replacement to your engine, and use your [serial number to identify the year](/blog/how-to-tell-what-year-a-ezgo-golf-cart-is).

## The 5-minute flow test

1. Make sure there's fresh fuel in the tank and the tank cap vent isn't blocked.
2. Pull the **outlet** hose off at the carburettor and point it into a small container.
3. Crank the engine for 2–3 seconds.
4. **Strong, pulsing squirts:** the pump works, so look at the carburettor or ignition.
5. **Weak dribble or nothing:** carry on with the checks below before you buy a pump.

Fuel is flammable. Do this outside, away from sparks, with the cart in neutral and the parking brake on.

## Before blaming the pump

Most "bad pump" cases turn out to be one of these:

- **Cracked, soft or collapsed pulse line.** An old line can look fine and still lose the pulse signal. It costs a few dollars to replace, so replace it first.
- **Clogged inline filter.** Cheap to replace and often decades old.
- **Air leaks on the inlet side.** A split pickup hose inside the tank or a loose clamp lets the pump suck air instead of fuel.
- **Blocked tank vent.** The engine runs for a few minutes, then starves as a vacuum builds in the tank. Loosen the cap and see if the problem goes away.
- **Weak crankcase pulse.** A worn engine, a stuck breather valve or a recent rebuild with timing or ring issues can produce too little pulse to drive even a new pump. Forum members chasing "three new pumps and still no fuel" usually end up here.

To check the rest of the system, briefly gravity-feed the carburettor from a small container above it, with care. If the engine then runs normally, the problem is upstream: the pump, pulse or lines.

## Replacing the pump

1. Disconnect the battery and let the engine cool.
2. Pinch off the inlet and outlet hoses with hose clamps to limit spills.
3. Note or photograph which hose goes where, then pull all three.
4. Unbolt the old pump and fit the new one **in the same orientation**.
5. Fit new clamps, reconnect the pulse, inlet and outlet hoses, and check for leaks before running the engine.

While you're in there, replace the fuel filter and the pulse line too. It costs little and saves you doing the job twice.
